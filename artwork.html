ARTWORK / SELF-PORTRAIT

Fragments of
becoming.

The people around us leave something within us. I wanted to explore what happens when those fragments become stone, and something living begins to grow through.

Illustrator · Photoshop · Code

Explore the process ↓

THE INSPIRATION

What makes us
who we are?

I think we carry fragments of the people around us. The things they say, the love they give, and the environments we grow up in become part of how we understand ourselves. Even when we feel like a single person, there are many experiences holding that person together.

My reading of Hegel makes me think about how deeply our lives are connected to a social world. We act within conditions we did not entirely choose. In this piece, I imagine those conditions becoming stone: the expectations, habits, and ways of being that can make us feel fixed in place. The repeated faces hold different positions, but they share that same fractured surface.

Yet I do not think that surface is all we are. Deep within us, there is something soulful that reaches toward love and toward God. The flowers are my way of giving that movement a form. Something delicate grows through a body that appears too rigid to change. What seems closed still holds the possibility of becoming.

Kierkegaard’s writing about faith, particularly the “teleological suspension of the ethical” in Fear and Trembling, gives me another way to approach that tension. I use it symbolically here: the inward life reaching beyond the shape the world has given it. I am interested in that difficult movement toward something we cannot fully explain from the outside.

For me, the flowers do not erase the fragments. They grow from them. I wanted the portrait to hold both truths at once: we are shaped by what surrounds us, and there is still something within us capable of reaching beyond it.

PROCESS STUDY

From a flat shape
to a living surface.

Drawing, image editing, and code each gave me a different way to work with the same idea.

  1. 01 / ILLUSTRATOR

    Give the portrait its structure.

    I began by drawing the portrait foundation in Illustrator, working with the outline of the head, the shoulders, and the features of the face. The foundation preview helps show how a few flat shapes can establish a face, and how repetition, scale, and rotation change the feeling of the composition.

  2. 02 / PHOTOSHOP

    Bring something living into the image.

    I imported the flower imagery and edited it in Photoshop. Bringing the flowers into the portrait gave me a way to connect the human figure with growth. Their movement contrasts with the stillness of the busts and opens up the space above each head.

  3. 03 / CODE & TEXTURE

    Turn the skin into stone.

    I used code to create the stone-like skin texture. The fractured surface makes the faces feel as though they have been assembled from separate pieces. In the finished image, those cracks carry the idea of a person shaped by many influences, while the flowers suggest something within that person continuing to grow.

  4. 04 / COMPOSITION

    Let the fragments speak together.

    The finished arrangement brings the faces, stone, and flowers together against a dark ground. Repeating the portrait at different sizes and angles lets me think about the same inner struggle across many versions of a self.

About the foundation study and the supplied code

The foundation preview is a reconstruction used to make the structure easier to see; it is not a recovered screenshot of the original drawing session. The accompanying Illustrator JSX creates named layers, editable clipping boundaries around sections of an existing JPEG, and an optional reconstructed vector foundation. It preserves original image pixels inside those boundaries.

The original Illustrator, Photoshop, and coded texture workflow above is my account of making the artwork. This layer-building script documents a later reconstruction; it does not contain the original stone-texture generator or recover the original authoring history.

READING BEHIND THE WORK

An interpretation in images.

Hegel · the self in a social world

Hegel’s account of freedom gives social relationships and institutions a constitutive role. The image of imposed conditions hardening into stone is my interpretation, rather than a quotation or a claim that Hegel saw society only as a restriction.

Read: Hegel’s Social and Political Philosophy ↗

Kierkegaard · faith and inwardness

In Fear and Trembling, the “teleological suspension of the ethical” concerns Abraham, his relation to God, and a paradoxical conflict with universal ethical duty. My flowers borrow that tension symbolically; the concept is not simply another name for personal growth or permission to disregard others.
