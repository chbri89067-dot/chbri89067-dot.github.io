01 / ANIMATED COLLAGE

Palace intrigue.

A crowded court, a rising sword, and an absence left behind.

THE IDEA BEHIND JP

What happens
behind the doors?

This idea grew out of my mother’s love of historical dramas, especially stories about palace intrigue. I was interested in the relationships inside a court: who holds power, who depends on someone else, and how an apparently beautiful setting can contain suspicion and danger. I brought together figures to represent different court roles—a shogun, an emperor, a chief retainer, a concubine, and others—because each has a place in that network of intrigue.

The palace’s sliding doors became a symbol of entry into that world. A door separates what can be seen from what happens behind it. I wanted the background to suggest that entering the palace also means entering its hidden relationships. Around the faces, I used red spider lilies as symbols of death, drawing on the Japanese symbolism that informed this idea. Their placement near the nose, ears, mouth, and eyes connects the senses to the intrigue: what people see, smell, hear, and say within the house.

The sword contrasts with the surrounding ornament and the indirect ways intrigue unfolds. Whatever is whispered or concealed, its consequences can become physical. The visible blade is a reminder that death may be the outcome of these relationships. In the final animation, the sword slowly rises from its sheath in preparation for an assassination. The screen goes dark, and when the scene returns, the shogun is gone. The missing figure tells us what happened during the darkness.

THREE STARTING POINTS

Different ideas.
A shared process.

I began with three collage ideas, labelled flowers, jp, and Try1. Each grew from a different personal connection. JP became the direction I developed into the final GIF.

A connection through flowers

This idea came from my mother’s love of ikebana. I wanted to touch on something meaningful to her through the arrangement of flowers and the space around them. The collage combines repeated landscape and botanical images with larger flowers and stems, bringing that personal connection into the foreground.

A court full of relationships

My mother’s interest in historical dramas led me toward palace intrigue. In this starting composition, I brought different court roles together within a setting of gold screens and sliding doors. The figures’ proximity creates the sense of a shared court, while their different positions suggest the relationships I wanted to explore.

Industry and a distance from God

Try1 began with my concern that industrialization has severed our connection with God. I was thinking about Enlightenment ideas of reason and progress, and what might be lost when industrial achievement becomes a measure of human life. By placing factories beside portraits and religious imagery, I wanted to make that spiritual distance visible.

FROM STILL IMAGE TO ANIMATION

Making the
absence matter.

I developed JP through different arrangements and colour treatments. The experimentation image records one of those variations: the red background, the floral details, and the sword make the danger more explicit. The final GIF returns to the gold palace setting and adds a sequence of events to the composition.

I used Photoshop layers and a custom JSX script to animate the sword while keeping the flowers, background, and remaining figures fixed. The blackout moves through the top, middle, and bottom of the picture, then holds before the character disappears. When the artwork fades back in, the empty place changes how we read the scene.

The timing became part of the idea. The slowly rising blade builds anticipation, the darkness conceals the act, and the pause makes the final absence something the viewer has time to notice. The GIF is the finished work; the three initial collages and the experimentation still show how I arrived there.

02 / SELF-PORTRAIT

Fragments of
becoming.

I think the people around us and the environments we live in become part of who we are. Through this portrait, I wanted to question how those influences can restrict us, and how something within us still reaches toward love and God.

THE INSPIRATION

What makes us
who we are?

I think an individual is constructed, in part, through the people around them. The expectations we encounter and the environments we grow up in affect how we act, but they also influence what we believe we are capable of doing. In this portrait, I wanted to give that relationship a physical form. Each face is made of fragments, because the self I am trying to represent has been shaped through many different influences.

My understanding of Hegel’s attention to social life led me to consider the conditions within which we act. Although we make decisions as individuals, we do so within a world that has already established expectations for us. I use stone to represent what happens when those expectations become restrictive: a person begins to take on a fixed shape, and moving beyond that shape becomes difficult. The stiffness of the bust gives this restriction a visible form.

However, if the conditions around us shape our actions, does that mean they determine everything we can become? I do not think they do. I believe there is a soulful nature within us that continues to grasp toward love and God, even when our outward form appears fixed. The flowers growing from the heads represent this movement. Their growth suggests that the individual still has the capacity to develop beyond the restrictions represented by the stone.

In particular, I connect this tension to Kierkegaard’s “teleological suspension of the ethical” in Fear and Trembling. His discussion concerns Abraham’s relation to God and the difficulty of understanding faith through universal ethical duty. My use of the concept is symbolic: I am interested in how an inward relation to God can exceed what is understandable from the outside. The flowers allow me to represent that inward movement within a figure whose surface otherwise appears rigid.

What remains difficult for me is the relationship between being shaped by others and becoming something beyond those conditions. The flowers still grow from the fragmented body, so the influences that construct the person remain part of the image. In that sense, I see becoming as an ongoing process. The portrait expresses my belief that we can grow beyond a fixed understanding of ourselves, while leaving open how much of that inherited shape we continue to carry.

PROCESS STUDY

How I built
the portrait.

I used Illustrator for the foundation, Photoshop to edit the imported flowers, and code to create the stone texture. Each part contributes to how I wanted the figure to be understood.

  1. 01 / ILLUSTRATOR

    Drawing the foundation

    I drew the portrait foundation in Illustrator, beginning with the head, shoulders, and facial features. In the preview, the eyes, nose, and shadows are separate flat shapes, yet their arrangement allows us to recognize a face. This makes the underlying structure easier to examine before considering the flowers or the texture.

  2. 02 / PHOTOSHOP

    Importing and editing the flowers

    I then imported the flower imagery and edited it in Photoshop. Placing the flowers above the open heads connects their growth to the individual. I wanted that connection to be visible, because the flowers represent something emerging from within the person.

  3. 03 / CODE & TEXTURE

    Creating the stone surface

    I used code to create the stone-like skin texture. The cracks divide the surface into smaller pieces while preserving the recognizable face underneath. This is where the process connects most directly to my interpretation: the individual appears to be constructed from fragments, and the stone gives those fragments a sense of stiffness.

  4. 04 / COMPOSITION

    Arranging the repeated portraits

    In the finished composition, the portraits repeat at different sizes and angles against a dark background. Although their positions change, the relationship between the fragmented faces and the flowers remains. I see that repetition as a way of returning to the same question about the self through different arrangements of the figure.

About the foundation study and the supplied code

The foundation preview is a reconstruction used to make the structure easier to see; it is not a recovered screenshot of the original drawing session. The accompanying Illustrator JSX creates named layers, editable clipping boundaries around sections of an existing JPEG, and an optional reconstructed vector foundation. It preserves original image pixels inside those boundaries.

The original Illustrator, Photoshop, and coded texture workflow above is my account of making the artwork. This layer-building script documents a later reconstruction; it does not contain the original stone-texture generator or recover the original authoring history.

READING BEHIND THE WORK

An interpretation in images.

Hegel · the self in a social world

Hegel’s account of freedom gives social relationships and institutions a constitutive role. The image of imposed conditions hardening into stone is my interpretation, rather than a quotation or a claim that Hegel saw society only as a restriction.

Read: Hegel’s Social and Political Philosophy ↗

Kierkegaard · faith and inwardness

In Fear and Trembling, the “teleological suspension of the ethical” concerns Abraham, his relation to God, and a paradoxical conflict with universal ethical duty. My flowers borrow that tension symbolically; the concept is not simply another name for personal growth or permission to disregard others.
